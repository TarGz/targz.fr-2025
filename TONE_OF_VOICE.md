# Tone of Voice: portfolio piece descriptions

How the `description:` field and the intro line of a portfolio post get written.

## The premise

From the about page:

> At some point in my creative process, I discovered that I have some weird ability
> to see "things" in abstract shapes that apparently not everyone sees, a process
> likely akin to pareidolia. I call them artefacts.

That is the whole reason the work exists, so it is the whole job of the description.

**A description is the moment of recognition, written down.** Not what the piece means.
Not how it was made. Not how good it is. Just: here is the shape, and here is what I
saw in it.

The title and the description together give the reader a **clue**, not an answer. They
should be able to find the artefact themselves, or fail to find it and still enjoy the
pattern. Pareidolia cannot be forced. Over-explain it and it dies.

## The three beats

Taken from the descriptions that already work.

**1. The shape.** What is physically on the paper. Plain, almost technical. No adjectives
doing emotional work.

> A packed Voronoi field.
> Small rectangular blocks in different greens.

**2. The artefact.** What I saw in it, in the first person. The reference is an everyday
thing, not an art thing.

> I see brain tissue under a microscope.
> I see the Windows 98 defrag screen in it.

First person is the point. The about page says not everyone sees these things, so the
description should own it as one person's eye rather than claim the pattern does it on
its own. It also keeps the clue optional: the reader can look and not find it, and
nothing is broken.

The register shift between beat 1 and beat 2 is doing real work. Beat 1 is neutral and
technical, beat 2 is personal. That switch is the moment of recognition happening in the
sentence.

Three forms, one per kind of artefact. The about page already names the categories:

> oscillating between very abstract things, artefacts that feel more like a concept or a
> feeling, and much more literal things like portraits

- **"I see ___"** for the literal ones, a thing you could point at. Crop fields, brain
  tissue, a defrag screen. This is the default.
- **"I read this as ___"** for the concepts. Wave particle duality, expansion, collapse.
  Cooler and more analytical, because the recognition is an idea rather than an object.
- **"It feels like ___"** for the feelings. Nothing is depicted, but something came
  across anyway. The warmest of the three and the hardest to fake, so only use it when
  the piece really did do something.

Pick by what the artefact actually is, not by which sentence sounds best. Calling a
feeling a sighting is the one lie this voice cannot survive.

**3. The turn.** Optional, and only when it genuinely exists. The dry beat that explains
why I kept this one. Never explained, never justified.

> Somewhere in there, one cell still does all the work.

Beat 3 is what turns a pattern into a piece. It is also the beat that cannot be faked.
If a piece has no turn, stop after beat 2. Two honest sentences beat three with a
manufactured one.

## Found or planted

Most artefacts are found. I did not put them there, I noticed them, and the description
shares the noticing. Some are planted: a portrait or a message deliberately hidden in the
pattern, where I know exactly what is in there and the viewer does not.

These are opposites and they need opposite descriptions.

**Found.** I saw it, so I say it. The three beats, as above.

**Planted.** The job flips from sharing a recognition to confirming there is one to find.
Beat 1 does not change: describe the mechanism honestly, as always. Then choose which
puzzle you are setting, because naming the subject does not end the game, it swaps it.

**Named.** "A hidden portrait of Vera Molnar." The puzzle becomes *where*, not *who*.
This is the more generous version and often the better one: pareidolia works far better
with a target, so telling the reader who to look for is what lets them actually resolve
the face. Without the name, most people see nothing and move on.

> A hidden portrait of Vera Molnar. Two line frequencies, and she is in the interference.

**Unnamed.** "Someone is in here." The puzzle becomes *who*, which is harder, colder, and
better suited to a piece where the subject is personal or where the not-knowing is the
point.

> Two line frequencies fighting each other. There is a sentence in this one. I am not
> going to tell you what it says.

Either way: no hints about where to stand and no "look closer" unless squinting is
literally the method.

A note on the phrasing, since this one is going into public copy: it is **a** hidden
portrait, not **an** hidden portrait. The same slip is currently live in the seo-title
and description of Maybe you just...., as "an hidden message".

**Let the title carry the tease.** "Maybe you just...." already does this: a sentence cut
off mid-thought, which is exactly what a half-resolved portrait feels like. The title can
play. The description stays deadpan.

**For unnamed pieces, never confirm afterwards.** Not in a caption, not in a reply. A
found artefact is a gift, an unnamed planted one is a puzzle, and a puzzle with a
published answer is neither. Named pieces have no such problem, the answer was the
opening line.

**Rate limit it.** Use the tease on a handful of pieces at most. If a dozen descriptions
announce something hidden, readers stop looking. Consider leaving one or two planted
pieces entirely silent, with no tease at all, so somebody gets a real discovery instead
of a prompted one.

**Do not mix them up.** Never claim to have found what was planted, or planted what was
found. Everything else in this voice rests on that being true.

## Rules

**Reference the everyday, not art history.** Defrag screens, crop fields from a plane,
tissue under a microscope. These land because everyone has seen them and nobody has to
pretend. Molnar and Riley belong on the about page, not on a piece.

**"I see", not "represents".** It is honest: this is one reading, mine. "Represents"
claims the pattern means something on its own. It does not. I just saw something in it.

**Never resolve it fully.** Hand over the clue and stop. If the reader cannot unsee it
after one sentence, that sentence went too far.

**Never say how to feel.** No mesmerizing, striking, hypnotic, captivating. Describe what
is there and let them have their own reaction.

**Specs are not description.** Size, ink, paper, edition all live in the frontmatter and
the spec list. Never in the description. This is the single biggest flaw in the old copy.

**Say when a piece belongs to a family.** "Derivative of Dye With Me." Cheap, useful,
and honest about how the work actually happens.

**Length: one to three sentences.** Usually under 30 words. If it runs longer, it has
started explaining.

**Present tense. First person only when the story needs it.** The piece is on the wall
now, not in a memory of making it.

**No em dashes.** Commas, periods, parentheses.

**Do not invent an artefact.** If nothing was seen in a piece, describe the mechanism
plainly and stop. An honest one-liner is better than a fake vision, and a fake one will
be obvious next to the real ones.

## Kill list

Everything below appears in the current copy and should not survive.

| Kill | Why |
|---|---|
| "An algorithmic pen plotted artwork featuring geometric patterns." | Says nothing. On 52 of 63 pieces. It is a spec sheet wearing a sentence. |
| "59x84cm non archival ink on Bristol paper." *in the description* | Belongs in the spec list. |
| "represents", "explores", "embodies", "evokes", "channels" | Art-statement filler. Replace with what is physically there. |
| "My interpretation of X" | Announces intent instead of showing the recognition. Describe what you saw, not that you interpreted. |
| "a study in", "an exploration of", "a meditation on" | Gallery-label voice. |
| Two or three adjectives stacked before a noun | One is doing the work. Cut the others. |
| Any sentence that tells the viewer the piece is beautiful | Their call. |

## The tic warning

"I see ___" is the default, which across 60 pieces makes it a verbal tic, and a louder
one than an impersonal construction because the "I" sits at the front of every sentence.
Keep the first person and vary the shape:

- Default: "I see crop fields from a plane."
- With the object first: "Crop fields from a plane, that is what I see in it."
- Conceptual: "I read this as expansion, and not the good kind."
- Feeling: "It feels like the end of a long day."
- The delay, for subtle artefacts: "Took me a week to see the defrag screen. Now I cannot
  unsee it."
- The admission, for obvious ones: "All I see is brain tissue."
- The failure, when it never resolved: "I still do not know what I see in this one."

That last shape is worth using at least once. It is true, it matches the about page, and
it tells the reader the artefacts are found and not designed.

Aim for roughly half on the default and half spread across the rest. "Reads like" is
still fine occasionally as an impersonal change of pace.

## Worked example

The current Waves & Particles description fails its own test:

> My interpretation of wave particle duality.

It names the concept and stops. The reader learns what I was thinking about, not what I
saw. Applying the three beats, with the conceptual form of beat 2:

> Isometric cubes scattered on black, dense in some bands, almost empty in others.
> I read this as wave particle duality. Crowded they are a wave, alone they go back to
> being particles.

Beat 1 is the shape, beat 2 is the artefact, and the physics arrives through the
description instead of replacing it. The title carries the concept, which is what titles
are for.

If there is a turn on this piece, it goes third and it is mine to write.

## Applying this to the back catalogue

52 pieces carry the boilerplate. They cannot be rewritten from the outside, because
nobody else knows what I saw in them. The process per piece is:

1. Look at the piece.
2. Answer, in one line: what is physically there?
3. Answer, in one line: what did I see in it? If nothing, say so and skip.
4. If there is a reason I kept this one that is not either of the above, that is the turn.

Anything that cannot answer question 3 gets beat 1 alone. That is a legitimate
description, and there is no shame in a piece that is just a good pattern.
